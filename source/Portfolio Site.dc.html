01 The problem
The business was losing roughly 6% of subscribers a month, and nobody knew who until they were already gone.
The retention team had no way to see risk in advance. Outreach was reactive — usually a discount email sent after someone had already asked to cancel, at the point where the decision was made and the conversation was about saving face rather than changing course.
The goal was narrow and testable: predict churn risk early enough that a retention offer could still plausibly work. Anything arriving after the cancellation request was, for this purpose, worthless.
02 The approach
01
Pulled and cleaned 18 months of customer usage, billing and support-ticket data.
02
Engineered behavioural features — login drop-off, ticket sentiment, plan downgrades, payment failures.
03
Tested Logistic Regression, Random Forest and XGBoost; picked XGBoost on the precision–recall tradeoff.
04
Handled class imbalance with SMOTE and threshold tuning rather than the default 0.5 cutoff.
05
Validated on time-based splits, so no future data was ever used to predict the past.

03 Key tradeoff
Precision over recall, because the two kinds of mistake cost very different amounts.
A false positive costs a discount email to someone who was never going to leave. A false negative costs an entire subscriber. That asymmetry sounds like an argument for recall — but the team had a fixed weekly capacity, and a list padded with low-risk accounts would push genuine risk off the bottom of it.
So the threshold was tuned for a list the team could actually work through, where most names deserved to be there. Recall was allowed to fall.

05 What I'd do differently
The weekly batch pipeline sets the floor on lead time. With more time I'd move the behavioural signals to real time — the model can see a login drop-off days before the batch job does, and those days are the whole product.
